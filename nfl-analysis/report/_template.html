The data doesn't.

Establish the run. Keep the defense guessing. Ice the kicker. Four things the whole sport takes as gospel, checked against a quarter century of every play ever charted. They don't hold up, and the coaches you would least expect come out looking smart.

01Offense

"You wear them down. Establish the run, impose your will, and by the fourth quarter, by December, they break."

Verdict: mostly myth, one grain of truth

Every run-first coach makes some version of that case, and it deserves a real hearing, not a stat dunk. So here it is, taken seriously and answered point by point against a quarter century of tape.

The next play

The usual version is about sequence: pound the run on first and second down, and the third down throw comes open. So we lined up every third down pass by how the offense got there.

Hold the third down distance fixed, because how you reach third down sets the yardage you face, and the pattern reverses the folk wisdom. At the medium distances that decide drives, teams that passed on first and second throw better on third than teams that ran twice. Run, run, then pass sits at the bottom of the group. Stacking more runs earlier in a drive doesn't lift the next pass either once the situation is held equal. Running first doesn't soften the defense. It just spends a down.

The long game

There is a bigger version too: grind it early and the pass opens up later, in the second half, in the fourth quarter, when it counts. That doesn't happen either.

Line up how much a team ran in the first half against how well it threw later, in neutral game states so a lead isn't quietly doing the work. The connection is nothing. Later in the same half, the second half, the fourth quarter: all flat. Flat even when the early runs actually gained yards. Flat for play-action, the one throw that supposedly needs a run game to sell it. Teams that ran the most early passed a touch worse late, not better.

The wear-down

Fine, says the coach, forget sequence. It is about attrition. Keep running and the front seven cracks by the fourth quarter. And the pass? The pass is a luxury. The run is the foundation.

Neither survives contact. Run efficiency is flat across all four quarters, and a team's fourth-quarter running is completely unrelated to how much it pounded the ball early. Nobody gets worn down. As for the run being the foundation, the tape says it is the other way around: runs work when the box is light, and a light box is something the passing game earns. The more a team throws, the fewer defenders it sees stacked against the run. You don't run to set up the pass. You pass to open up the run.

December

Here is where the coach finally lands a punch. In the cold, you have to be able to run it. And the data mostly backs him.

Passing is a big edge in a dome or in September. Drop the temperature and that edge melts. Below 25 degrees the advantage of throwing over running falls by three-quarters, down to a rounding error, and in the coldest games it is no longer distinguishable from zero. Heavy wind does the same thing. So the frozen-field coach is right that the gap closes. He is just wrong about the last step: the passing edge shrinks toward zero, but it never actually flips. Throwing stays the marginally better bet even in the deep freeze, and teams barely change their run-pass mix to match.

02Play-calling

"The great play-callers keep a defense guessing."

Verdict: backwards

The flip

It runs the other way. Account for the situation a caller faces, and the most predictable ones run the best offenses.

Raw numbers say unpredictable teams score more, but that is a mirage. Good offenses simply live in friendlier down and distance, where the run-pass split is naturally balanced. Compare each caller to the league in the exact same spots and the sign flips. The clearest case is the man the booth needles for it: Andy Reid is the easiest elite caller to predict, and he runs the single best offense in football. It also holds up as more than a quarterback effect, and it is a real trait, not a one-year blip.

Where it pays

So should a caller ever go off script to stay unpredictable? The data says the payoff is a gradient, and surprise pays nowhere.

Being predictable helps most on early downs, where committing to what works matters more than disguise. The edge is smaller on obvious passing downs and vanishes in the thin late-and-short samples. But no situation rewards being harder to guess. On early downs, Andy Reid and Joe Brady are both near the top for predictability and near the top for offense, well clear of the league. The early down edge survives a quarterback quality control too, so it is not just star passers.

03Special teams

"Call timeout, freeze him, ice the kicker."

Verdict: no signal

Twenty-six seasons and 2,221 pressure kicks in, icing shaves maybe three points off a make. The catch is that you cannot tell that apart from doing nothing at all.

Control for distance, kicker quality, weather and the moment, and icing is worth about 2.9 fewer points of make probability, with a confidence interval that runs straight through zero. Score every coach's personal icing record against luck and 20 of 21 land inside the noise cone. The one that pokes out, Pete Carroll, is exactly the single fluke you would expect from 21 tries. Nobody owns the ice.

04Game management

Two ways to waste a timeout, and who does it most.

Verdict: names named

A timeout gets wasted two ways: burned early to escape a play clock jam, or left on the table, still in hand at the gun of a one-score loss. Different coaches, different habit.

Mike Martz burned them at twice the league rate in every season he coached. Norv Turner walked off 35% of his close losses with a timeout unused, against a league mark of 15%. And the twist the booth never mentions: Andy Reid, forever roasted for clock management, almost never leaves them unused, 4 times in 61 close losses, the best rate of any long-tenured coach.

The thread

Football's folk wisdom is mostly about looking decisive. The math rewards something quieter.

01The run doesn't set up the pass, and defenses don't wear down. If anything, passing is what opens up the run. The one exception is the frozen field, where the gap finally closes.
02Predictable isn't the weakness. The best callers commit to the right call, dare you to stop it, and it holds up even after you strip out the quarterback.
03Icing the kicker is theater. A quarter century of kicks can't separate it from luck.
04Timeouts get wasted in public. The tape shows who burns them and who leaves them on the table.
