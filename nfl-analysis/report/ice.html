Call timeout. Freeze him. Make him think about it. Twenty-six seasons of pressure kicks say the whole ritual does nothing at all.

Verdict: no signal
The effect that isn't

Three points, and a shrug

There have been 2,221 pressure field goals since 2000: the final two minutes of a half or overtime, with the kick tying the game or taking the lead. Defenses iced 585 of them.

Compare only the kicks where the defense actually had a timeout to spend, which kills the obvious confound, and control for distance, kicker quality, weather, era and the moment itself. Icing is worth about three fewer points of make probability.

The best estimate leans the defense's way. But after a quarter century of kicks, you still cannot tell it apart from doing nothing.

The coaches

Nobody owns the ice

Maybe it works for someone. Some coach with a feel for the moment.

Score every coach's personal icing record against what pure luck would produce, and 20 of 21 land inside the noise cone. Not close to the edge. Inside it.

The trap

The Pete Carroll problem

One dot sits outside the cone. Kickers made just three of the eight Carroll iced. He ices rarely, so it reads like a coach who picks his spots and cashes in.

Eight kicks is the entire case. One was a 64-yard heave with a one-in-three chance no matter what anyone called.

Being choosy is not the secret either. Across all coaches, how selective you are has no bearing on whether your icing works: the correlation is +0.01. Kyle Shanahan ices nearly three times as often as Carroll and watched all twelve of his go through.

Run the whole thing as a coin flip, twenty thousand simulated seasons where the timeout changes nothing, and the luckiest of 21 coaches still matches Carroll's mark about once a decade. He is not the exception to the rule. He is the reason you have to check.

The verdict

Icing the kicker is the most-televised piece of strategy in football that does not appear to do anything. Twenty-six seasons, 2,221 pressure kicks, every control we could apply, and the effect still cannot be separated from noise.

It is not that it definitely does nothing. It is that if it does something, a quarter century of the best-recorded league in sports has not been enough to prove it. Coaches keep calling it at the same rate they always have.
