## ACT 3 — MYTH TWO: HE CAN'T PLAY HURT

### `A3_RawSplit` — the number that looks damning

> So let's take the second one seriously, because at first glance it's not close.
>
> True shooting percentage — one number for how efficiently a guy scores, counting
> threes and free throws properly.
>
> Healthy Embiid in the playoffs: six-forty-six.
>
> Hurt Embiid: five-fifty-nine.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> That's eighty-seven points of true shooting. To put that in context — that is roughly
> the distance between the best offence in the league and one of the worst. Not one
> player. The whole offence.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> And that number is wrong.

### `A3_Correction` — two corrections

> Here's the first problem. There are only eleven healthy games in the entire sample.
> And four of them came against the 2021 Washington Wizards.
>
> Washington had no rim protection that series. None. Embiid shot seven-fifty. It's the
> best mark of his playoff life, and it is not a repeatable basketball outcome — it's a
> matchup.
>
> Take that series out.
>
> Six-forty-six becomes six-oh-two. Seven games left. And notice where that lands — it's
> now *below* his own regular season, not above it. The whole "healthy playoff Embiid is
> better than regular-season Embiid" idea was one soft series.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Second problem, and this one's bigger.
>
> Every star gets worse in May. They stop playing the bad teams. They start seeing
> defences built specifically for them. Embiid's career playoff true shooting is
> five-seventy-eight against six-thirteen in the regular season — and that's across
> every health state. Healthy, hurt, all of it.
>
> So if you compare hurt playoff games to a healthy regular season, you're measuring the
> injury *and* the playoffs at the same time, and calling the whole thing the injury.
>
> Subtract the playoff tax from both sides. Whatever's left is the health tax on its own.
>
> *(hold — the number lands)*
>
> Thirty-eight points. Not eighty-seven.
>
> Eighty percent interval runs from minus sixty-six to minus two. Resample the games
> twenty thousand times and the hurt ones come out worse in ninety-two percent of them.
>
> So the direction is solid. The size is not. Eleven healthy games will not pin this down
> any tighter, and anyone who gives you a precise number here is guessing.

### `A3_Defense` — the null, and the honest part

> That's the offence. What about the other end?
>
> Embiid is a three-time All-Defensive centre. He finished second for Defensive Player of
> the Year. If playing hurt is dragging him down, it should show up in the rim
> protection.
>
> Per thirty-six minutes — blocks, healthy: one-forty-six. Hurt: one-eighty-six.
>
> Defensive rebounds go the other way. Steals go the other way.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> He blocks *more* shots hurt than healthy. Which points the wrong way for the story I
> was trying to tell.
>
> Now. The temptation here is enormous. You've got a counterintuitive finding, it's
> shareable, it makes you look smart. "Actually, hurt Embiid is a *better* defender."
>
> Before you do that — check whether this test could have found anything at all.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> To register a difference in block rate, this sample would need a gap of one-point-two-two
> per thirty-six. Against a career rate of one-seven-nine.
>
> That's a sixty-eight percent swing. For steals it'd need a hundred and twenty-nine
> percent.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> This test has no power. It could not tell a hurt Embiid from a healthy one unless the
> difference were enormous — and nothing here is enormous.
>
> So I'm not going to tell you his defence holds up under injury. I'm going to tell you
> his defence under injury is **unmeasured**. Not unaffected. Unmeasured. Those are
> different sentences and only one of them is true.

## ACT 4 — THE TURN

### `A4_Scoreboard` — the screenshot number

> Efficiency is a proxy anyway. Wins are the thing.
>
> Philadelphia's playoff record, sorted by what Embiid was that night.
>
> Healthy: nine and five. Six-forty-three.
>
> Hurt: twenty-three and twenty-nine. Four-forty-two.
>
> Not playing at all: six and six. Five hundred.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Read the bottom two rows and Philadelphia looks like a better team with Embiid on the
> bench than with Embiid at eighty percent.
>
> That number is going to get screenshotted. And it's almost certainly wrong.

### `A4_Confound` — the actual finding

> Split it by round.
>
> In first rounds, he's six-forty-three healthy and six hundred hurt. That's a difference
> of one game. One.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Now second rounds. Thirty-two games. Eleven and twenty-one.
>
> Every single one of them amber.
>
> *(long hold)*
>
> Joel Embiid has never played a healthy second-round game. Not once, in ten years.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Which means in his own data, health and round can't be separated. Every healthy game he
> has is a first-round game. So when you control for one, you're always controlling for
> part of the other.
>
> There's no clever regression that fixes this. No amount of arithmetic gets around it.
> The data simply doesn't contain the comparison everybody wants to make.

### `A4_Everybody` — the reframe

> And here's the thing I got wrong at the start.
>
> Jaylen Brown started all eleven games of a title defence on a partially torn meniscus
> that Boston was publicly calling an impingement.
>
> Tyrese Maxey played an entire postseason in a splint with a torn tendon in his shooting
> hand, then re-jammed it in Game 2 and said afterward he couldn't trust the ball out of
> a trap.
>
> Nobody has ever painted their squares amber.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Amber is not unique to Embiid. Everybody is hurt in May. Two of his new teammates just
> finished entire playoff runs in it.
>
> The difference isn't *whether* he's hurt. It's what the hurt costs.
>
> Brown at eighty percent gives back a step off the bounce. Embiid at eighty percent
> gives back the offence — because he *is* the offence.

### `A4_TheRed` — the reversal

> *(grid returns, amber and bone drain out, red outlines sharpen)*
>
> So the thing that has actually cost Philadelphia is not the amber.
>
> It's the twelve red squares. The games he wasn't there for at all.
>
> And the fact that he has arrived at every second round of his career already hurt.

### `A4_Stakes` — the close

> Which is worth something you can put a number on.
>
> The market has Philadelphia around twelve percent to win the title. That price already
> has his injury history in it.
>
> Guarantee him healthy every night, and that goes to somewhere between nineteen and
> forty-one percent. Between one-point-seven and three-point-seven times their current
> odds.
>
> Roughly half of Philadelphia's championship equity is sitting on one man's body.
>
> *(hold)*
>
> Over a twenty-game title run, the model expects him to start sixteen. About one and a
> half at full health.
>
> One and a half.
>
> *(long hold)*
>
> An oddsmaker put it more briefly than I've managed in ten minutes: the Sixers will go
> as far as Joel Embiid's health takes them.
