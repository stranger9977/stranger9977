What a battle actually is

A depth chart is not a document. It is a time series. Every day, the league's charts get rewritten, and almost all of that movement is noise — a trade, a signing, a cut. Somebody arrives, somebody leaves, the list reshuffles, and nothing was contested.

Strip those out and what remains is the only thing that counts as a camp battle: two players who were both already on the roster, and one of them passed the other. No transaction. No new arrival. Just a job changing hands.

Since May 1 the league's charts recorded 1,106 changes. Once arrivals and departures are removed, 806 of them were actual competition — and those resolve to distinct contested slots, because when two players trade places the chart records it twice.

One thing this can't tell you

battles that beat writers wrote about never moved the depth chart at all. The tempting read is that the coverage invented them. That read is wrong, and the data says so.

Sort those by side of the ball and the pattern is structural: of covered defensive battles never register, against of offensive-line battles. That gradient isn't media behavior — it's chart resolution. A public depth chart lists DE, DT, LB, CB, S. It has no row for the nickel, no row for the third safety, no row for a rotational edge. The fight can be completely real and still have nowhere to appear.

So the claim runs one direction only: there are real competitions nobody is covering. The reverse needs a source that can see defensive sub-packages, and a public depth chart can't.
